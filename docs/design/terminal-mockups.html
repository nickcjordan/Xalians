Xalians design direction, working demo, revision 5

One relay, many terminals

Every panel below shows the same creature record, Hypnopet of Telypso, rendered on a different machine the lore supports. Each one now says exactly what object you are looking at, which parts of it are painted metal, which are paper, and which are a screen. Writing that down exposed one inconsistency shared by five of the six panels and by the live site. It is fixed below.

The umbrella concept

The lore already has an object that connects many different machines into one view: the Zolto rebuilding the QED network, the galaxy's only instant link, while Kozrak tries to keep the galaxy in the dark. That is xalians.com. The site shell, the navigation, the sign-in and the account, is a relay. Each area you navigate to is a remote terminal the relay has patched you into. The nav is the one constant object. Everything behind it is allowed to be a different piece of hardware from a different faction and era.

In design-system terms there is a core layer nobody may change per area and a material layer each area sets. Element hues sit in the core because fire is red on every planet.

The medium rule

Describing each terminal as a physical object forced one question: where does variable information live? A hull is painted once at the factory. It can carry the word "INDEX" but it cannot carry "#00015", because the next creature has a different number. The live site prints creature data in monospace directly on the matte hull, and so did five of my six panels. That is the inconsistency. Every terminal has exactly three media, and every piece of content belongs to one of them.

The anchoring rule

The medium rule says what each thing is. It is not enough to say it; the object has to show how it is attached to the world, or the viewer cannot tell a surface from a screen from empty space. So every object carries the cues of its own physics: paper is clipped, pinned or lying askew, and it ages; a screen sits in a bezel with screws and a curved highlight; keys sit in a recessed bank and have travel; a board has an edge, mounting holes and cables; a counter has a lit top edge and a dark front lip. None of these cues carry information. All of them carry belief.

Hull · painted once, matte, never emits

Fixed legends, key caps, lamps, bezels, asset plates, warning livery. Anything that is identical for every creature and every visitor.

"SPECIMEN RECORD", "GENERATE", the ECHELON asset number
Paper · printed once, matte, variable, committed

A record that was produced and will not change: a minted creature's slip, a ledger docket, an archive card. Typewriter or line-printer face. Stamps and colored tabs are how paper carries state and element color. You cannot press paper, so paper never has buttons.

the docket, the index card, a printed genome slip
Screen · live, variable, the only thing that emits

Anything that can still change: a record being generated, a link status, a battle in progress, a cursor. Where a terminal has a color raster CRT, the record can live there in full color. Where it has only an amber or green readout, the record must go to paper.

the CRT on the field terminal, the ledger line, the relay packet

A. Baseline, what ships today

Instrument Panel

A slab of olive-enamelled steel bolted into a hangar wall. Brass bezels, bakelite keys, a small green CRT set into a hole in the panel. The design system's own words are "Star Wars by way of Fallout", but the materials are pre-plastic, so it reads as a WWII field radio or a submarine's control room rather than either.

Hull
Everything you see. Legends are silkscreened; the brass rivets in the corners are real fasteners.
Paper
None.
Screen
Only the small green CRT, showing four status lines.
Left as is, for comparison. The inconsistency: the creature's name, index, origin, height, mass and both meters are printed on the hull as if silkscreened at the factory. The specimen portrait is a colored tile "behind glass" in a housing, which implies a photograph or a lit panel that nothing else in the design accounts for. On the live site this is how the species page, the generator record and the account page all work today.

B. Salvaged ECHELON-era hardware, my recommendation

Field Terminal

A portable survey unit the size of a suitcase, built by an ECHELON company for its field biologists before the End Wars. It opens like a luggable computer: a gunmetal case, an off-white enamel face with the keys and lamps molded in, and a color raster CRT under dark glass filling most of the face. A one-line amber vacuum-fluorescent strip above the CRT is the unit's own status voice. The company is gone. Whoever has it now has patched it, taped it, and pointed it at a Generator.

This is the Alien Nostromo register: cassette futurism with corporate livery, worn in. No 1950s Americana anywhere. Warm and scrappy because of the wear and the amber, not because of the palette.

Hull
The gunmetal case and enamel face. Fixed legends in an extended-width face, the company name, the asset number, the reconditioning tape, the keys.
Paper
Only the tape and the asset label. A printed slip could eject from a slot when a creature is minted; not shown.
Screen
The color CRT carries the whole record: name, element blocks, portrait, specs, meters. The amber strip carries battery, link and job state.
Changed. In revision 1 the record was printed on the enamel face and a small amber CRT showed status only, which is the same hull inconsistency as panel A. Moving the record onto a color CRT also answers a question I had dodged: how a monochrome amber screen could show fourteen element colors. It cannot. So the unit has two displays, a color raster for the record and an amber strip for its own voice, which is exactly how period hardware split those jobs. Revision 3 adds the case: hinge knuckles along the top where the lid folds down, four screws holding the enamel face onto the chassis, and a thick dark CRT bezel with a curved-glass highlight so the screen sits inside the face rather than being painted on it.

C. Kozrak's arena bureaucracy

Registry

A clerk's counter at the Valleron arena. You are standing on the public side. The counter is black lacquer trimmed in gold leaf, the regime's colors, with a one-line amber ledger display set into it and two heavy keys. On the counter, clipped into a holder, is your combatant's docket: cream stock, typed on a typewriter, red margin rule, a red stamp when the fee clears. The docket is the record. The counter is the machine that accepts your entry.

This is the Empire side of the Star Wars split: cold institutional power expressed as paperwork. Nothing here is friendly, and nothing is broken either. Kozrak's machines work.

Hull
The lacquer counter, the gold rule, the two keys, the red lamp.
Paper
The docket carries everything about the combatant: typed name and fields, a black-and-white identification photograph, element marks as inked stamps, stats as printed bars, and the admission stamp.
Screen
The single amber ledger line: entry number, fee state, bracket. Only the things the clerk's machine is tracking right now.
Changed twice. Revision 2 put the record on paper, but the docket floated on black with nothing saying whether the black was a surface, a screen, or nothing. Revision 3 adds the cues a real counter would have: a steel clip pinning the docket down, the docket sitting very slightly askew under it, a lit top edge and a dark front lip on the counter so it reads as a slab with depth, the amber readout recessed into a bezel with two brass screws, and the two keys mounted in a recessed key bank. Revision 4 remakes the keys, which still read as flat digital buttons: each is now a moulded cap with a lit top edge, a dark side wall five pixels deep, and a shadow onto the counter, sitting in its own black socket with a thin brass rim. The red key is red bakelite with cream engraving, the black key is black bakelite with gold engraving, and the well carries the window number in gold.

D. Poseidas, the neutral ground

Archive

A reading desk in the Poseidas Deep Archive, the library of the one faction the lore codes as courts, commerce and science. The room is dim. The desk is dark sea-slate, and the only light is the warm pool of a reading lamp falling on the middle of it. An archivist has pulled a catalogue card from the drawers and set it in that pool: card stock that has yellowed over decades, foxed with brown spots, darker at the edges where a thousand hands have held it, typed on a typewriter whose ink has gone soft, with a pencil cross-reference added later in the archivist's hand. The photographic plate is pasted on with black corner mounts and has gone sepia. The colored tabs on the top edge are the element classification, the way real card catalogues color-coded subjects, and they have faded with the card. The round hole at the bottom edge is where the drawer's rod ran through it. The desk has two enamel keys for requesting the next record.

So to answer the question directly: the cards are literal paper cards on a physical desk, not a picture of cards on a screen. There is no screen on this terminal at all. A reading room does not need one, and its absence is what makes the archive feel like a different place from every other machine on the site.

Hull
The desk surface, the archive's name strip, the two request keys, the revision line printed on the desk edge.
Paper
The card: accession number, name, one-line summary, pasted plate, typed fields, stats as printed bars, colored classification tabs, the pencil note, the rod hole.
Screen
None. The archive is the one terminal without a live display.
Changed twice. Revision 2 fixed the medium problem but you were right that it read as a hospital index card printed this morning: bright white stock, crisp type, a light clinical ground with no lamp anywhere. Revision 3 ages the card (yellowed stock, foxing, worn edges, faded typewriter ink, sepia plate with corner mounts, a pencil annotation, the rod hole) and turns the room down: a dark slate desk with one warm lamp pool, so the card is the lit thing rather than the whole panel. The desk keys now have real travel. The archive is still the warmest and lightest terminal, but it is lit, not white.

E. Zolto rebel signal station

Relay

A hand-built relay unit on a bench somewhere on Zolton. There is no proper case, just a sheet-steel cover plate screwed over the electronics, with louvred vents cut low where the warm parts sit. A salvaged color tube in a steel frame is bolted to the plate on four standoffs, one black cable runs from its connector down into the plate beside the keys, and a strip of tape reads "trace 3 cut, do not open." The signal bars in the corner are the QED entanglement to a paired unit on another world. Kozrak's sweeps are why it is on a bench and not in a building.

This is the Rebel side of the Star Wars split, and the only terminal that is honestly improvised rather than manufactured. Its accent is the crimson of Zolton's bloodstorms, the lightning that makes entanglement possible.

Hull
The steel cover plate, its screws and vents, the tube frame and standoffs, the cable, the tape, the keys.
Paper
None. A rebel relay does not print.
Screen
The CRT carries the record and the packet state together, because on a relay the record is a packet in transit.
Changed. Revision 1 set the creature's name in a display face directly on the board and hung a color plate off it, the hull inconsistency again. Now the board carries only silkscreen and the record lives on the CRT, with the packet header and sweep countdown in the same screen because that is what a relay operator would actually see. Revision 3 tried to make the board a board with an edge line and four faint rings, and it did not work. Revision 4 drew the whole circuit board, chip, capacitors and bodge wire, which read as physical but was too much. Revision 5 puts a sheet-steel cover plate over the electronics: six cross-head screws, louvred vents, a stencilled unit name, one visible cable from the tube to the keys, and a tape label saying what was fixed and not to open it. The tube keeps its frame and standoffs. The improvised feel now comes from the plate and the tape rather than from exposed parts.

F. The machine speaks for itself

Readout

Not a terminal you operate. This is what a Generator says when any of the other terminals points at it. Canon treats Generators as autonomous, with their own sensors and their own judgment, so the Readout has no legends, no keys except one to acknowledge, and no color of its own: paper-white text on dark glass, in the machine's monospace voice. It is a mode, not a place. On the field terminal, the CRT switches to this while a creature prints, then hands back.

Hull
A bare bezel and one key. Whatever terminal is hosting it.
Paper
None, though the moment it finishes is the moment a slip would print on the host.
Screen
Everything. Stats as plain bars, no element color until the host terminal takes the record back and shows it in its own register.
Changed. Only the bars, which were block characters and did not line up. They are now drawn bars in the same paper-white so the machine's voice stays monochrome. Revision 3 gives the glass a rounded bezel and a curved highlight so it reads as a tube, not a black rectangle.

Which terminal fits which area

A starting assignment, all of it overridable. The rule: match the machine to who, in the fiction, would own that part of the experience.

Area of the siteTerminalWhy, in the fictionMaterial
Shell: nav, sign in, accountRelayThe connection itself. The Zolto network is the only thing that reaches every other machine.
Encyclopedia: story, worlds, bestiary, codexArchivePoseidas is the neutral seat of science and arbitration. Reading is calm; paper under a lamp, not screens; the warmest place on the site.

What I need from you

Does the medium rule hold?Hull for fixed legends, paper for committed records, screen for live state. It changes the live site more than any color choice would, because today variable data is printed on the hull everywhere.
Which panels feel like Xalia to you, and which feel wrong?In your own words. "B but less orange" or "C is too much paperwork" is exactly the right level.
How much wear?B carries tape and scuffs. The rest are clean. Wear everywhere fits the used-universe stance but costs legibility and is easy to overdo.
Is a screenless, paper-only encyclopedia acceptable?D is the biggest departure from the current site and also the thing that would most separate reading from playing. It is now a lamp-lit card on a dark desk rather than a white page.
Does the anchoring rule earn its cost?Clips, screws, bezels and lips are pixels that carry no information. They are what make the medium rule believable, and they are also how a design starts to look overdone.
