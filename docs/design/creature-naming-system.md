# Xalian Creature Naming System

## Purpose

A creature receives its name after its defining pieces exist. The name is an abstract compression of the creature, not the seed from which the body and behavior are reverse-engineered and not a recital of registry fields.

Most Xalian names should carry one or more recognizable roots related to the creature. The relationship may be direct, transformed, or tonal. A minority may be spirit-first coinages, as Figzy is playful and whimsical, but those names still need an intentional sound and a documented reason.

The system protects two things at once:

1. **Fit:** the name feels connected to this creature once its derivation is known.
2. **Variety:** the roster does not collapse into one house style of compounds, pseudo-Latin endings, repeated animal suffixes, or gratuitous X and Z spellings.

## When naming happens

Keep the creature unnamed through the target brief and the dependency design. Use a descriptive target label or temporary key while developing it.

Coin the final species name only after these pieces are stable:

- home world and ecological function;
- material and body plan;
- silhouette and defining anatomy;
- movement and sensory identity;
- feeding and social behavior;
- trait and archetype center;
- the signature's plain physical act;
- the intended emotional register.

Concept art may reveal a better name. If the visual design materially changes the silhouette, movement, or emotional register, rerun the name pass rather than protecting an early favorite.

The signature title is coined after its instrument, action, medium, baseline behavior, and intensity band are fixed. It follows the grander signature register and is a separate decision from the species name.

## The name brief

Before proposing names, write a compact brief with five inputs:

| Input | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Defining act | What does the creature do that another species does not? |
| Material or anatomy | What is physically memorable about how it does that? |
| Ecological role | Why does this organism exist in its world? |
| Temperament | What does encountering it feel like? |
| Sound intent | Should the name feel playful, severe, patient, uncanny, swift, massive, delicate, or something else? |

Extract a root palette from those answers. Roots may come from ordinary English, established scientific terms, or older language roots a general reader can pronounce. A root is inspiration, not an obligation to preserve its spelling.

Do not begin with an element word plus an animal word. That construction remains available when it genuinely fits, but it is not the default.

## Candidate modes

Build six to twelve candidates across at least three modes. This forces meaningful variation before preference takes over.

### 1. Transparent compound

Two recognizable ideas remain readable on sight. Existing surface examples include Drilltail, Graviclaw, Chromocat, Hypnopet, and Terragoyle.

Use when immediate readability is part of the charm. Reject when it merely restates element plus body part.

### 2. Fused roots

Two related words overlap, compress, or trade syllables. Existing surface examples include Newtapede and Thirstaserp.

Use when the roots should be recoverable without the name sounding like a label.

### 3. Transformed root

One strong technical, ecological, or behavioral root is altered into a creature name through clipping, vowel movement, consonant change, or a non-semantic ending.

Use when a creature has one unusually precise central process. The alteration must improve rhythm and identity rather than add decorative letters.

### 4. Tonal coinage

Sound carries the first impression and roots are secondary or deeply transformed. Figzy demonstrates the permission for a playful spirit-first name.

Use sparingly. The proposal must state the intended feeling and explain why a more literal name would weaken it.

### 5. Hybrid

A recognizable root is combined with a tonal echo, a second obscured root, or a creature-like cadence. This mode is useful when the name should reward explanation without requiring it.

## Sound and spelling audit

Every finalist is read aloud and compared with the full roster. Record:

- likely plain-English pronunciation and syllable count;
- opening sound and final sound;
- hard, soft, clipped, flowing, heavy, or buoyant cadence;
- length and visual silhouette on the page;
- shared prefixes, suffixes, or conspicuous letter patterns with existing names;
- whether a reader can say it after seeing it once;
- whether spelling depends on decorative punctuation or gratuitous rare letters;
- whether it sounds too close to an existing creature, world, institution, substance, or major character.

No single opening letter, ending, syllable count, or naming mode is banned. Repetition becomes a problem when several recent additions lean on the same device or when two creatures become difficult to distinguish in speech or print.

Species names remain one word unless a future concept gives a compelling reason to change the roster convention. Apply plain English pronunciation rather than inventing a hidden pronunciation rule.

## Meaning audit

A finalist must pass all of these questions:

1. Can its roots or tonal logic be explained in one or two sentences?
2. Does that explanation refer to defining facts rather than incidental traits?
3. Would the name still fit if the element label were hidden?
4. Is the connection loose enough to feel like a name rather than a database label?
5. Does it avoid promising an ability, anatomy part, temperament, or world role the creature does not have?
6. Does it avoid Earth-fauna shorthand unless the design deliberately belongs to the roster's transparent-compound tradition?

## Selection record

Each full creature proposal records:

- the five-part name brief;
- the root palette;
- six to twelve candidates across at least three modes;
- the leading candidates rejected and why;
- the selected derivation;
- pronunciation and sound intent;
- roster-diversity findings;
- exact and case-insensitive repository collision results.

This is a decision record, not an algorithm that selects the highest numerical score. A name may pass every mechanical check and still be dull. Final judgment favors memorability, fit, and roster variety.

## Signature-title pass

Signature titles follow a different register from species names:

- derive from the completed physical act, its consequence, or the image it creates;
- sound grander than ordinary generated ability names;
- need not encode instrument, action, and medium because those fields already exist;
- avoid generic adjective-plus-attack constructions when a more particular image is available;
- avoid possessives, hyphens, non-ASCII punctuation, franchise language, and real-world weapon names;
- check the exact title against every catalog cell, neutral pool, and existing signature.

Generate several candidates in distinct approaches: physical image, consequence, declaration, mythic metaphor, and transformed technical language. Select only after comparing their spoken rhythm beside the other signature titles.

## Review cadence

After each ratified creature, add its naming mode, opening sound, ending, syllable count, and derivation to the roster audit. Before naming the next creature, review at least the most recent five additions and the species on the same world or element.

If the process repeatedly produces the same sound or spelling, change the candidate modes being explored. Do not distort an individual creature merely to satisfy a naming quota.
